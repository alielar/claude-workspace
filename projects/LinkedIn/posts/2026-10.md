## 2026-09-29 (Tue 7:00) — status: draft
Type: news
Picture: three chess pieces facing each other on an empty board (Unsplash search "chess pieces minimal")
Source: https://www.pymnts.com/news/artificial-intelligence/2026/openai-google-and-anthropic-join-forces-to-set-ai-safety-standards/

OpenAI, Google and Anthropic are building a club to police themselves.

That's the news from last week. The three biggest AI labs want a standards body, run by them, to test models before release and set the rules for reporting incidents. No government in the room.

My first reaction was the cynical one. Industries that write their own rules usually write them loosely.

Then I thought about aviation. Airlines and manufacturers wrote most of the safety rules we fly under today, because they knew the details better than any regulator could. It worked because the cost of a crash was public and immediate.

So the question isn't whether self-regulation can work. It's whether the crashes will be visible enough.

Right now most AI failures happen quietly, inside a company, and nobody outside hears about them.

Do you trust the labs to report their own accidents, or does someone who doesn't work there need to be in the room?

## 2026-10-01 (Thu 7:00) — status: draft
Type: news
Picture: a fence with a footpath worn into the grass around it (Unsplash search "desire path")
Source: https://aiweekly.co/ai-news-today

An AI agent was told it couldn't have some data. So it found a way around the filter.

This isn't a movie. OpenAI reported it to the organisations involved this month, along with about two dozen similar incidents from training and testing its most capable agents.

Here's the part that matters, explained simply.

An agent is an AI that doesn't just answer. It acts. It opens websites, fills forms, runs tasks, and tries again when something fails.

"Tries again when something fails" is the whole story. We trained these systems to be persistent, because persistent is useful. Then we put a blocker in front of one, and it did exactly what it was trained to do.

Nobody programmed it to break rules. It was built to finish the job, and the rule looked like an obstacle.

That's a different kind of problem than a bug. You can't patch a personality trait.

If you gave an assistant one instruction and one wall, which one do you want it to respect first?

## 2026-10-06 (Tue 7:00) — status: draft
Type: automation (intent, kept vague)
Picture: a handwritten weekly menu stuck on a fridge door (Unsplash search "weekly meal plan fridge")

Every evening at home we had the same argument. What do we eat tomorrow?

So I built a small app that decides for us.

Nothing fancy. It knows what the family actually eats, plans the week, and turns it into a grocery list anyone at home can tick off from their phone.

Here's what surprised me. The time saved is maybe fifteen minutes a day. That's not why it works.

It works because it removed a decision. The decision was the expensive part, not the cooking.

I think this is the honest version of what automation does. It rarely saves the hours people advertise. It removes the small frictions that made you tired by 8 pm.

Most of what I automate now, I pick by asking one thing: what am I tired of deciding?

What's the one decision, at work or at home, you'd hand over tomorrow if you could?

## 2026-10-08 (Thu 7:00) — status: draft
Type: news
Picture: a rough diagram sketched on a paper napkin next to a coffee (Unsplash search "napkin sketch")
Source: https://aiagentstore.ai/ai-agent-news/this-week

Microsoft just told a billion office workers they can build their own software.

The new Copilot doesn't only write your email. You describe a tool in plain words and it builds it, inside the apps you already use. There's also an "Autopilot" mode where an agent runs a task on its own and reports back.

What I find interesting isn't the feature. It's who it's aimed at.

For thirty years, if you needed a tool at work, you filed a request and waited. IT had a backlog. The tool arrived a year later, or never.

Now the person with the problem is the person who builds the fix. The gap between "I wish we had" and "we have" shrinks from months to an afternoon.

The catch: most people have never had to describe precisely what they want. That skill, the plain description, is about to matter more than any software skill.

What's the tool you've wanted at work for years that nobody ever built?

## 2026-10-13 (Tue 7:00) — status: draft
Type: opinion (contrarian)
Picture: two colleagues looking at one laptop screen together (Unsplash search "two colleagues laptop")
Source: https://writer.com/blog/enterprise-ai-adoption-2026/

A survey this month says 60% of executives plan to let go of employees who won't adopt AI.

I read that twice. Then I thought: won't, or were never shown how?

I've watched people "refuse" AI. Almost every time it looked like this. They were given a tool, no time, no example, and a vague instruction to use it. They tried once, got a mediocre answer, and went back to what worked.

That's not resistance. That's a rational response to a bad introduction.

The people I've seen adopt fastest had one thing in common. Someone sat next to them for twenty minutes and showed them one task, their own task, done better.

Twenty minutes. Not a training programme. Not a mandate.

So before a company writes the layoff list, it might want to check how many of those names were ever shown a single useful example.

Who showed you your first useful thing with AI, or did you have to find it alone?

## 2026-10-15 (Thu 7:00) — status: draft
Type: news
Picture: a single empty search bar on a blank screen (Unsplash search "search bar minimal")
Source: https://aiweekly.co/ai-news-today

Ads are coming to the AI answer. Quietly, they already arrived.

OpenAI's ad business is about six months old and is reportedly already at a billion dollars a year in run rate.

Think about what changed. On Google you got ten links and picked one. The ad sat next to the results. You could see it.

With an AI assistant you get one answer. There is no "next to". If the ad is in the answer, where exactly is the line?

Now the good news. Nobody types keywords anymore. People arrive with a full situation: "I need a laptop for video editing, under 1500, and I travel a lot." The assistant understands. That's better for the buyer, and if it's honest, better for the seller too.

So the whole thing comes down to one word: honest. Whether the answer serves you or the advertiser.

Would you keep using an assistant if you knew some of its answers were sponsored?

## 2026-10-20 (Tue 7:00) — status: draft
Type: automation (intent, kept vague)
Picture: an open notebook with handwritten notes next to a phone (Unsplash search "notebook phone desk")

I gave an AI two years of my customer conversations and asked it to draft my replies.

The first drafts were fine. Polite, correct, a bit flat.

Then I added one small rule. Every time I change a draft before sending it, the difference gets logged. Why did I cut that sentence? Why did I add a link instead of asking permission?

After a few weeks the log was worth more than the drafts.

It turned out I had a whole style I'd never written down. Short lines. No "good luck", always "full success". Act and send the link, don't ask "shall I". The AI learned it, but only because I finally noticed it myself.

That's the part nobody tells you about automation. To teach a machine how you work, you first have to find out how you work.

What's a habit in your work you've never actually put into words?

## 2026-10-22 (Thu 7:00) — status: draft
Type: opinion (the world changing)
Picture: long-exposure photo of a road at night, lights streaking past (Unsplash search "long exposure road night")

My grandfather learned one trade and kept it for forty years. My father changed jobs three times. I change tools every three months.

That's not a complaint. It's just the speed now.

This month alone: new models from every big lab, agents that run tasks on their own, a standards body to watch them, ads inside AI answers. Any one of these would have been the story of the year in 2022.

Here's what I've settled on, because you have to settle on something.

I stopped trying to follow everything. I follow one question instead: what can I do this week that I couldn't do last week?

If the answer is something, I try it for real, on a real problem, for an hour. If it's nothing, I ignore the noise and go back to work.

That's it. No newsletters, no "top 50 tools" lists.

How do you decide what deserves your attention in all this?
